When you ask three suppliers for a quotation on "soap and detergent", you may get three different HSN codes back. That is not always a mistake. The Harmonized System (HS) splits washing products across two neighbouring headings, 3401 and 3402, and a few products sit awkwardly between them. This guide explains the split in plain terms so you can read a quotation, check an invoice and brief your customs broker with confidence.

GoodBiz Hygiene is a merchant exporter: we source soaps, detergents and home-care products from vetted Indian manufacturers and handle the quote, documents and shipment. The notes below are general guidance for buyers. They are not customs advice, and your own country's tariff has the final word.

## What an HSN code is, and why importers care

HSN stands for Harmonized System of Nomenclature, the international product classification run by the World Customs Organization. The first six digits of a code are the same in almost every country. Each country then adds its own further digits for national tariff lines. India's export classification (ITC-HS) uses 8 digits, and your import tariff may use 8, 10 or more.

The code matters for three practical reasons:

- **Duty and taxes.** Your customs authority applies duty and VAT or GST according to the code on the import declaration.
- **Permits and standards.** Some countries require registration, labelling approval or a conformity certificate for particular headings.
- **Consistency.** The invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and bill of lading should all describe the goods the same way. A mismatch is a common cause of questions at the port.

## The core split: soap versus everything else

The simplest way to remember it:

- **3401** is for **soap**, and for certain skin-washing products that are not true soap, if they are in the forms the heading names.
- **3402** is for **organic surface-active agents** (other than soap), plus the **washing and cleaning preparations** made from them.

"Soap" here has a specific meaning: the product of reacting fats or oils with an alkali, such as bars of toilet soap and laundry soap. Detergents work differently. They use synthetic surfactants, so they are classed separately even though they clean in a similar way.

### Heading 3401: soap

The subheadings you will meet most often are:

- **3401 11**: soap for toilet use, in bars, cakes or moulded pieces. This includes bath soap, herbal soap, glycerin soap and hotel guest soap.
- **3401 19**: other soap in bars or cakes, such as laundry soap and some dish wash bars.
- **3401 20**: soap in other forms, such as flakes, granules and soap noodles used as a raw material.
- **3401 30**: organic surface-active products and preparations for washing the skin, in liquid or cream form and put up for retail sale. Liquid hand wash and body wash usually belong here.

### Heading 3402: detergents and cleaners

This is the larger heading for non-soap cleaning chemistry. Typical subheadings are:

- **3402 31 and 3402 39**: anionic surface-active agents, which includes raw materials such as LABSA and SLES.
- **3402 50**: washing and cleaning preparations put up for retail sale. Detergent powder, liquid detergent, dish wash liquid, floor cleaner and similar products are usually placed here.
- **3402 90**: other preparations, often used for bulk and institutional cleaners that are not put up for retail sale.

## Where buyers get tripped up

### "Put up for retail sale"

Several subheadings turn on whether the goods are packed for retail. A 500 ml bottle on a supermarket shelf is retail. A 200 litre drum for an institutional user or a re-packer is not. The same liquid can therefore land on a different line depending on the pack. When you quote for both retail and bulk packs of one product, expect two codes on the paperwork and ask for both.

### Liquid skin wash: soap or not?

Liquid hand wash is not soap in the chemical sense, yet it sits in heading 3401 because that heading explicitly includes liquid and cream skin-washing products for retail. A product with a medicated or cosmetic claim may be treated differently in your country, so tell us the claim you plan to print on the label.

### Mixed-use products

Some products do two jobs, for example a cleaner that also disinfects. Customs authorities look at the product's essential character and the claims made for it. If a product could reasonably sit in two headings, the label, the composition and the intended use usually decide.

### Six digits versus eight

A six-digit code such as "3402 50" is shared worldwide. The extra digits are national. In our quotations we show the six-digit code, and the 8-digit ITC-HS code is confirmed per order, because it depends on the exact pack and form. Your broker will then map the six digits to your own tariff line.

## A simple checklist before you order

1. **Write down the product form.** Bar, liquid, powder, paste, flakes or raw material.
2. **State the pack.** Retail pack or bulk, and the pack size.
3. **State the claims.** Antibacterial, herbal, disinfecting, whitening or none.
4. **Ask the exporter for the proposed code.** Request it on the quotation and the proforma invoice.
5. **Check it against your tariff.** Your customs broker or the tariff database in your country confirms the duty line.
6. **Keep all documents aligned.** The same description and code should appear on the invoice, packing list and certificate of origin.

- 3401 is soap and certain retail skin-washing products. 3402 is non-soap surfactants and the washing and cleaning preparations made from them.
