Documents are where many first-time cleaning product imports go wrong. The goods are fine, the price is agreed, and then the container sits at the port because one paper is missing or one description does not match another. This guide lists the documents you will usually meet when importing soaps, detergents and cleaners from India, and what each one is for.

GoodBiz Hygiene is a merchant exporter: we source from vetted Indian manufacturers and prepare the shipping paperwork for each order. Requirements vary by destination country and by product, so everything below is general guidance. The exact set is confirmed per order, and your customs broker has the final word on what your port will accept.

## The core shipping documents

Almost every shipment needs these, whatever the product.

### Commercial invoice

The commercial invoice is the seller's bill and the main document customs uses to value the goods. It shows the buyer and seller, product descriptions, quantities, unit prices, total value, the agreed trade term and the HSN code. Importers use it to calculate duty and taxes, and banks use it under a letter of credit. The description must match the other papers word for word. For help with the code, see our guide to [HSN codes 3401 and 3402](/blog/hsn-code-3401-vs-3402-soaps-detergents/).

### Packing list

The packing list explains what is inside each carton, pallet or drum: number of packages, net and gross weight, dimensions and batch or marks where relevant. Customs officers use it to check a physical inspection against the invoice. A clean packing list also helps your warehouse receive the goods quickly.

### Bill of lading

The bill of lading is issued by the shipping line. It is a receipt for the cargo, a contract of carriage and, in its original form, the document of title that lets you collect the goods. Ask early whether you need originals, a telex release or a sea waybill, because this affects when you can clear the cargo and how payment is handled. Name, address and notify party details must be exact.

## Documents that prove origin and quality

### Certificate of origin

A certificate of origin states the country where the goods were made. Importing countries may require it as a customs formality, and some use it to decide whether a lower duty applies.

There are two broad types:

- **Non-preferential (general) certificate.** It confirms the country of origin only. It is commonly issued by a chamber of commerce or an authorised body.
- **Preferential certificate.** Where a trade agreement between India and your country gives reduced or zero duty, a preferential certificate in the format named in that agreement may be needed. The goods must meet the agreement's origin rules, and the format and issuing body vary by agreement.

Do not assume you qualify. Ask your broker whether a preferential rate exists for your tariff code, and tell us before the order so the right certificate can be arranged.

### Certificate of analysis (COA)

A COA is a laboratory report on the specific batch you are buying. It lists the test parameters and the results against the agreed specification. The parameters depend on the product. For a surfactant such as [LABSA](/products/labsa/), they typically include active matter, free sulphuric acid, free oil and colour. For a detergent powder they may include active matter, pH, bulk density and moisture. For a liquid cleaner they may include pH and appearance.

A COA is only useful if the specification was agreed first. Before you confirm an order, ask for the specification sheet and agree which parameters and limits will appear on the COA. Keep the COA together with a retained sample from the same batch in case of a later dispute.

### MSDS or SDS

A safety data sheet describes the product's composition, hazards, safe handling, first aid, fire-fighting measures, storage and disposal. SDS is the current name and MSDS the older one, but both mean the same thing. Your warehouse, your transport company and, in some countries, your customs or environmental authority will ask for it. Request the SDS in the language and format your market needs, since some countries expect a specific layout.

## Dangerous goods and chemical cargo

Not every cleaning product is dangerous goods, but some are. Strongly acidic products, corrosive liquids and certain surfactant raw materials can be classed as hazardous for sea transport.

If a product qualifies, expect:

- **Dangerous goods declaration.** A signed declaration of the product's UN number, class, packing group and packaging details.
- **Hazard labels and markings.** Correct labels on drums, cartons and the container.
- **Approved packaging.** Drums and IBCs that meet transport requirements.
- **Carrier acceptance.** Shipping lines approve dangerous goods separately and may charge a surcharge or limit the vessels available.

Examples include acid-based cleaners such as [toilet bowl cleaner](/products/toilet-bowl-cleaner/) in standard acid grades, and bulk raw materials such as LABSA. Milder grades may be treated differently, and the classification depends on the exact formulation and on current transport rules. Tell us if you need a mild grade or if your port has restrictions, and we will confirm the classification with the manufacturer before booking.

## Destination-specific registrations and approvals

Beyond the standard set, many countries add their own requirements. These are the buyer's area to confirm early, because they can take weeks.

- **Product or importer registration.** Some countries register cleaning products, or the importer, before goods may enter.
- **Conformity or pre-shipment inspection.** Certain markets require a certificate of conformity or an inspection before shipment.
- **Label approval.** Language, hazard wording, symbols, net content and importer details may all be regulated.
- **Standards and testing.** A few countries ask for testing to a national standard.
- **Import licences.** Some chemicals need a licence or a permit from a local authority.

We cannot register a product in your country on your behalf unless this is agreed in advance, and we do not assume that a requirement from one market applies to another. Ask your authorities or broker for the current list, and send it to us at enquiry stage.

## What does not apply

Cleaning products are chemicals, not agricultural goods. A phytosanitary certificate is for plants and plant products, so it is not normally needed for soaps, detergents or cleaners. Packing materials, such as wooden pallets, may have their own treatment marking rules, so check these with your broker.

## A practical document checklist

Use this list when you plan an order.

| Document | Who issues it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Seller | Customs valuation and payment |
| Packing list | Seller | Inspection and warehouse receiving |
| Bill of lading | Shipping line | Proof of shipment and title to the cargo |
| Certificate of origin | Authorised body | Origin proof and possible duty reduction |
| COA | Manufacturer's laboratory | Batch quality against specification |
| SDS | Manufacturer | Safe handling, transport and storage |
| Dangerous goods declaration | Seller or manufacturer | Required for hazardous cargo |
| Local registrations | Your authorities | Entry permission in your country |

## How to avoid delays

1. **Send your document list at enquiry stage.** Include the broker's checklist, any letter of credit terms and any registrations.
2. **Agree product descriptions and HSN codes before invoicing.** Mismatches between documents are a common source of questions.
3. **Check letter of credit wording carefully.** Banks reject documents that differ from the credit by even small details, so review the terms with your bank before the order is confirmed.
4. **Agree the specification before production.** The COA should show what you asked for.
5. **Confirm dangerous goods status early.** It affects packing, carrier choice and cost.
6. **Ask for draft documents before shipment.** Check names, addresses, weights and descriptions while corrections are still easy.

## Terms and payment

FOB, CIF and CFR available; payment terms agreed per order (T/T or LC). The trade term you choose affects who arranges and pays for freight and insurance, and so which party handles certain documents, so settle it before the quotation is finalised.
