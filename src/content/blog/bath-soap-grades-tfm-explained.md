Open any soap quotation and you will see the letters TFM, often next to "Grade 1" or "Grade 2". For a first-time buyer these can look like technical jargon. They are in fact the quickest way to compare two bars that look identical on a photo and sell at very different prices. Understanding them helps you choose the right quality for your market, avoid paying for a grade you do not need and avoid disappointment when the first container arrives.

## What TFM means

TFM stands for Total Fatty Matter. Soap is made by reacting fats and oils (vegetable oils, palm-based oils, tallow or their derivatives) with an alkali, a process called saponification. The result is a mixture of soap, water and other ingredients. TFM measures how much of the finished bar, by weight, is fatty matter, meaning the fatty acids that do the real work of cleansing.

A simple way to picture it: if a 100 g bar has a TFM of 75%, then about 75 g of it is fatty matter. The rest is mostly water, plus glycerin, salt, fragrance, colour, titanium dioxide for whiteness and any added fillers or functional ingredients.

Three practical effects follow from this:

- **Lather and cleansing.** More fatty matter generally means richer, creamier lather.
- **Hardness and wear.** High-TFM bars are usually firmer and last longer in the wet soap dish. Lower-TFM bars often soften and wear faster.
- **Cost.** Fats and oils are the main cost in soap. A higher TFM needs more of them, so price rises with grade.

## How grades are used in trade

Many soap buyers use grades as shorthand for TFM bands. In Indian trade, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) specification for toilet soap defines grades by minimum TFM, and quotations frequently borrow that language.

The values below are commonly used reference figures for minimum TFM, not guarantees. Confirm the exact grade, the standard version and the test method with the manufacturer for each order.

| Grade | Commonly used minimum TFM | Typical positioning |
|---|---|---|
| Grade 1 | 76% min | Premium, long lasting, rich lather |
| Grade 2 | 70% min | Mainstream quality |
| Grade 3 | 60% min | Economy, price-led markets |

You will also hear suppliers use "premium" and "economy" without a number. That language is loose. Always ask for the TFM percentage in writing. ## Why TFM is not the whole story

TFM is a useful first filter, but a bar is more than one number.

### Moisture and weight

Soap contains water, and the amount varies. A bar that is 100 g when it leaves the factory may weigh less after weeks in a hot, dry container. Ask for the moisture and volatile matter figure, and agree whether the quoted weight is at packing or at delivery.

### pH and mildness

Soap is naturally alkaline. Typical bar soap measures around pH 9.5 to 10.5 in a 1% solution. Buyers targeting sensitive skin often ask for a mildness message, which needs supporting data rather than a slogan.

### Free alkali and chloride

Specifications often limit free caustic alkali and chloride (salt) content. These affect skin feel and storage behaviour. They are normally listed on the manufacturer's specification sheet.

### Appearance and finish

Colour consistency, surface finish, edges, embossing and fragrance stability all affect how the bar looks after months on a shelf. A test sample and a pre-shipment check help here.

## Matching grade to market

There is no universally best grade. The right choice depends on who buys the soap and what they pay.

### Premium and supermarket ranges

Retail chains and brand owners positioning on quality usually want the higher bands. A firm bar with strong lather supports a higher shelf price and repeat purchase.

### Mass-market and wholesale

In price-sensitive markets, especially where soap is sold by weight or in small multipacks, a mid or economy grade can be the commercial choice. The bar still works, but wears faster, and the pack must make honest claims.

### Hotels and institutions

Hospitality buyers care about small weights, consistent appearance and branding, so the grade is often secondary to bar size and wrapping. Our range includes [hotel guest soap](/products/hotel-guest-soap/) for this segment.

### Herbal and medicated bars

Herbal and antibacterial bars add functional ingredients to the soap base. For medicated bars, active ingredients and permitted claims vary by destination, so confirm them before you finalise a label.

## Reading a soap specification sheet

Request a sheet that states at least the following:

1. **TFM (%)** and the test method used.
2. **Grade** and the standard it refers to.
3. **Moisture and volatile matter (%)**.
4. **pH** of a 1% solution.
5. **Free alkali and chloride limits.**
6. **Bar weight and tolerance**, plus dimensions.
7. **Ingredients list** in the format your market requires.
8. **Shelf life** and storage advice.

If a supplier cannot provide these, treat the quotation with caution.

## Soap noodles and the grade question

Bars are shaped from soap noodles, which are the base material. TFM is usually discussed at the noodle stage too, and finished bars inherit it. ## Labelling and classification

Bath soap in bars is normally classified under HSN 3401 11 at six digits, and the Indian export code is confirmed at eight digits per order. Your market may also require ingredient lists, net weight marking and language requirements. Tell the exporter your destination early so the manufacturer can plan the packaging and printed wrapper.

## Key takeaways

- TFM shows how much of the bar is true fatty matter, and it largely drives lather, hardness and cost.
- Grades are shorthand for TFM bands. Reference minimums are commonly 76% min, 70% min and 60% min, but always confirm them per order.
- Ask for the actual TFM percentage and test method, not just the words "premium" or "Grade 1".
- Check moisture, pH, free alkali and bar weight alongside TFM.
- Choose the grade for your customer and price point rather than assuming the highest is best.
